The DJI Agras T100 frame-connector listing is a mixed structural-parts catalog. Its 16 selectable callouts include left- and right-hand frame connectors, landing-gear hardware, a crossbar, a spray-tank mounting piece, a buckle hook and cover, a cable clamp, an anti-collision pad, a rubber hole plug, and several fasteners. Treating the listing as one interchangeable “frame connector kit” would obscure the load path, installed position, handedness, and inspection evidence that should drive a repair order.

This guide turns the catalog into a controlled identification and triage workflow. It explains how to document a structural complaint, separate primary load-bearing evidence from retention and protection items, preserve left/right and callout identity, and prepare an order for technical approval. It does not provide a disassembly sequence, torque values, thread-locking requirements, fastener reuse limits, structural straightening instructions, or return-to-service authorization. Those requirements must come from current DJI service information and a qualified repair process.

## Why this parts group requires structural discipline

The T100 is not a light consumer airframe. DJI publishes a basic empty weight of 75 kg, a maximum payload of 100 kg at sea level, and maximum takeoff weights of 175 kg with two sprinklers or 177 kg with four sprinklers. These specifications do not provide design loads for an individual connector, but they explain why apparently small gaps, cracked interfaces, loose fasteners, distorted latches, and cable-contact conditions deserve controlled inspection rather than cosmetic judgment.

A frame-connector case can begin with several different observations:

- a hard landing, rollover, vehicle contact, or transport impact;
- a visible gap, shifted crossbar, or left/right asymmetry;
- a loose, missing, corroded, or damaged fastener;
- a buckle that does not close, retain, or release normally;
- a tank or payload interface that no longer seats consistently;
- a cable clamp that has released, allowing chafing or reduced clearance;
- an anti-collision pad or hole plug that is missing or deteriorated;
- recurring looseness after an earlier repair;
- unexplained vibration, rubbing, witness marks, or unusual landing stance.

None of those observations identifies a single catalog variant by itself. A displaced cable clamp may be secondary to a shifted structural interface. A broken hook cover may coexist with a distorted hook. A loose landing-gear fastener may have damaged the mating hole or connector. A visible gap can reflect incomplete seating, trapped debris, deformation, damaged hardware, or a mismatch between the installed part and the intended position. The useful question is not “Which item looks closest in the diagram?” but “What evidence defines the damaged interface and every part that transfers, retains, protects, or routes through it?”

## Divide the area into five inspection branches

The 16 options become more useful when grouped by the function of their installed interfaces. A good inspection follows each branch and then examines how the branches interact.

### 1. Left and right frame-connector branch

Callout 8 is the right frame connector and callout 16 is the left frame connector. Handedness is a controlled identifier, not a visual guess. Start every evidence package with aircraft orientation: photograph the complete aircraft from the front and rear, label the side, record the affected location, and include a wide view before taking close-ups. A cropped image can be mirrored by software, captured from underneath, or detached from the aircraft, making left/right interpretation unreliable.

DJI's recommended maintenance table instructs users to inspect the M1-M4 aircraft arm connectors, locking screws, and contact surfaces with the middle frame for cracks or misshapen parts. It also specifies checking whether the gap between an aircraft arm and its connector exceeds 2 mm, in which case the corresponding material is to be replaced, and checking that the middle-frame locking screws are tightened. That published 2 mm criterion applies to the aircraft-arm connector inspection named in the manual. Do not automatically transfer it to every gap around the frame-connector product listing.

For the selected connector area, record gap measurements at defined, repeatable locations rather than writing “slightly open.” Use a scale in the photograph, identify the measurement plane, compare the opposite side only as supporting evidence, and preserve the original images. A symmetric appearance is not proof of serviceability, but a side-to-side difference can help locate a shifted interface.

### 2. Landing-gear load path

The landing-gear branch includes the C-type connector (4), top crossbar (5), landing-gear hole plug rubber (12), associated screws, and the larger landing-gear structure outside this listing. A hard landing can transmit load beyond the first visibly damaged item. Inspect the foot, leg, crossbar, connector, fastener locations, surrounding structure, and ground-contact evidence as one path.

DJI's maintenance schedule calls for a monthly check of landing-gear fixing screws for looseness or breakage and a 12-month inspection of the landing gear for cracks. Those are minimum scheduled checks described in the user manual, not instructions to wait for the interval after an impact. Any event that may have overloaded the structure warrants an event-driven inspection before further flight.

Do not straighten a bent crossbar, dress a damaged hole, add a washer, or enlarge a mounting hole based on catalog information. Such actions can alter alignment, edge distance, contact, or load transfer. Record dents, buckling, fretting, coating disturbance, cracked paint, elongated holes, pulled threads, and displaced rubber. Escalate the assembly when approved acceptance limits are unavailable.

### 3. Buckle and retention branch

The buckle hook cover (13) and buckle hook (15) are separate orderable parts. A broken cover may be protective damage only, or it may indicate that the hook or mating latch absorbed an impact. Inspect the hook profile, pivot or attachment area, mating feature, secondary engagement, surrounding connector, and evidence of partial closure.

The T100 maintenance table calls for routine inspection of the middle-frame buckle and the aircraft-arm lock lever. It identifies misshaping, cracks, secondary-latch engagement, and lever stiffness as relevant conditions. The public product page does not provide the recommended stiffness value or an adjustment procedure. Therefore, “it feels tight” and “it closes if pushed harder” are not release criteria. If engagement, geometry, or stiffness is abnormal, keep the aircraft out of service until the approved procedure and limits are available.

### 4. Tank-mount and cable-routing branch

The spray-tank top mounting piece (7) and payload cable clamp (10) serve different functions, but both affect an installed payload interface. Check whether the tank seats without forcing, whether mounting faces are clean and undamaged, whether the lock or retention system operates normally, and whether the cable follows the intended route with adequate clearance through the full permitted movement of nearby parts.

DJI's pre-flight checklist requires parts to be mounted securely and cables to be connected correctly and firmly, explicitly including the spray tank and arm locks. The same checklist requires the payload system to be damage-free and functioning. A replacement clamp is not a complete cable repair. If a cable escaped retention, inspect its insulation, connector, strain relief, bend points, and every surface it could have contacted. Polished areas, flattened insulation, exposed reinforcement, cuts, chemical residue, or pinching require technical evaluation.

Do not use an improvised tie, adhesive mount, or changed routing simply because it prevents visible movement on the ground. Routing also has to remain clear during folding, unfolding, tank installation, vibration, steering on uneven ground, and normal structural deflection.

### 5. Pads, plugs, and fasteners

The anti-collision pad (9) and landing-gear hole plug rubber (12) may appear secondary, but their condition can reveal contact, aging, compression set, chemical exposure, or incomplete assembly. Inspect the mating surface when either item is missing. A new pad can hide a witness mark; a new plug can conceal moisture, residue, debris, or damaged edges. Document the condition before cleaning or replacement.

Fasteners at callouts 1, 2, 3, 6, 11, and 14 must be controlled by exact identifier and installed position. The catalog names do not authorize a torque, tightening sequence, lubricant, locking compound, or reuse decision. Record the fastener head and surrounding witness marks before removal. Bag and label removed hardware by callout and location. If a fastener was loose, inspect the mating threads and contact surfaces rather than treating re-tightening as a complete diagnosis.

## Use an event-driven structural inspection sequence

The following workflow is designed to improve evidence quality without pretending to be an internal repair procedure.

1. **Quarantine and identify the aircraft.** Record the T100 serial or fleet ID, configuration, payload, time or cycles if tracked, affected side, and date. Remove it from service when structural integrity or retention is uncertain.
2. **Preserve event evidence.** Save the operator report, app warnings, flight record, photographs of the site, touchdown attitude, transport restraints, and any recovered pieces. Do not discard a fractured cover or fastener.
3. **Make the aircraft safe.** Power down, remove the battery under the approved process, control chemical exposure, and unload the structure before close inspection. Do not work beneath an unsupported aircraft or loaded tank.
4. **Decontaminate appropriately.** Agricultural residue can hide cracks and create handling hazards. Use the approved cleaning method for the affected materials and preserve meaningful deposits or leak traces before cleaning.
5. **Photograph the complete geometry.** Capture front, rear, left, right, top, and landing stance on a level reference. Include the affected assembly and the opposite side, but do not use the opposite side as the sole dimensional standard.
6. **Trace the load path.** Follow the suspected force from ground contact or payload mount through the leg, crossbar, connector, buckle, frame, and adjacent interfaces. Mark every disturbed fastener, shifted witness mark, gap, crack, rub point, or deformed cover.
7. **Inspect routed systems.** Check the payload cable clamp and any cable, hose, or connector crossing the damaged region. Look beyond the broken clip for secondary abrasion, pinching, stretched routing, or connector loading.
8. **Measure only against defined criteria.** Record gaps and alignment in a reproducible way. Apply the 2 mm criterion only to the aircraft-arm connector gap addressed by the DJI maintenance table, not to unrelated joints.
9. **Build the parts hypothesis.** Map each confirmed damaged item to its callout, material number, exact option title, handedness, store SKU, quantity, and evidence photograph. Keep uncertain items listed as open questions.
10. **Obtain the repair specification.** Before disassembly or installation, obtain the applicable torque, sequence, thread treatment, reuse, alignment, inspection, and functional-test requirements from current approved service data.

This sequence intentionally separates inspection from repair. A complete parts order is not a repair disposition, and a mechanically assembled airframe is not automatically ready to fly.

## Distinguish four common evidence patterns

| Field observation | What to document first | Likely catalog branch to identify | Why the order should wait |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aircraft sits unevenly after landing | level reference, feet and legs, crossbar, both connectors, fastener positions, impact direction | 4, 5, related fasteners, possibly 8 or 16 | The first bent-looking part may not be the only loaded interface |
| Visible gap near a connector | exact location and measurement plane, crack/deformation check, locking condition, comparison view | 8 or 16 plus exact adjacent hardware | A gap needs an applicable limit and cause; appearance alone does not define the replacement scope |
| Buckle will not engage normally | hook and cover, mating feature, secondary engagement, surrounding geometry, contamination | 13 and 15 | Replacing the cover cannot correct a deformed hook, mating part, or frame interface |
| Cable has escaped its clamp | full route, insulation, connector faces, strain relief, contact marks, nearby movement | 10 plus any damaged cable or connector outside this listing | A clamp restores retention only if the routed system remains serviceable |
| Tank mount requires force to seat | empty and unloaded interface, mounting faces, lock operation, left/right alignment, debris | 7 and adjacent connector structure | Forced assembly can mask misalignment and transfer load to another component |
| Missing pad or plug | original location, residue, wear pattern, mating surface, chemical exposure | 9 or 12 | The missing item may be a symptom of contact, aging, or incomplete earlier work |
| One screw repeatedly loosens | exact screw code and position, threads, hole, contact faces, vibration/impact history | 1, 2, 3, 6, 11, or 14 as identified | Replacing or tightening hardware without finding the cause may repeat the condition |

These patterns are triage paths, not fault isolation by symptom. The repair decision must explain how the observed condition, applicable inspection limit, material identity, and adjoining interfaces support the selected scope.

## Convert the catalog into an auditable order

Every requested line should include:

- aircraft model and fleet or serial identifier;
- product-page URL and the date it was checked;
- numbered image callout;
- complete material number;
- exact catalog name;
- store SKU shown for the selected option;
- left/right orientation where applicable;
- installed location and quantity required;
- evidence photograph or inspection-record reference;
- technician approval and any unresolved inclusion question.

An auditable line might read: “DJI Agras T100, right side, callout 8, `YC.JG.ZS005949`, Frame Connector (Right), store SKU `YC.JG.ZS005949`, quantity 1, damage photos STR-04 through STR-08, pending confirmation of included fasteners.” “T100 connector” is not specific enough.

Callouts 3 and 14 require extra care. Both show material number `YC.ST.LL000730` and the same English screw description, while the store variants use SKU suffixes `-4` and `-5`. Preserve the callout and SKU context in the request. Do not claim that the suffixes represent different engineering revisions or physical dimensions unless the seller or approved parts data confirms it.

For callouts 8 and 16, include both the word “Right” or “Left” and an aircraft-orientation photograph. Do not let a mirrored mobile-phone image determine the order. For assemblies such as the crossbar, mounting piece, connector, or buckle hook, ask whether the sales unit includes hardware, pads, plugs, covers, or any preinstalled subcomponent. If the quotation bundles additional items, compare the bundle against the approved parts hypothesis and document the change.

## Receiving inspection and configuration control

When the shipment arrives, compare the supplier label, packing record, and physical item against the approved order before opening protective packaging unnecessarily. Verify model context, callout, material number, name, SKU or seller reference, handedness, quantity, and declared package contents. Photograph the label and part in the received condition.

Quarantine the item if:

- the material number is missing, incomplete, or different;
- a left/right marking conflicts with the order;
- a fastener is visually similar but not traceable to the required code;
- a connector, hook, crossbar, or mount shows impact, corrosion, distortion, or finish damage;
- rubber is cut, permanently deformed, contaminated, or aged;
- the package contents differ from the quotation;
- the part appears used or altered without an approved condition statement.

Physical fit is not sufficient evidence of compatibility. A screw can enter a threaded hole while having the wrong length, material, coating, or strength. A mirrored connector can appear close until surrounding interfaces are loaded. A hook can close without achieving the required engagement. Resolve documentation discrepancies before installation.

## Return-to-service evidence must close the original risk

Installation should be performed by a qualified technician using current approved data. The work record should identify every installed material number, removed item, fastener disposition, required torque or process reference, inspection result, and person performing or approving the work. If the repair data requires new hardware, seal replacement, curing time, alignment tooling, or staged tightening, record compliance rather than writing only “replaced connector.”

After assembly, the acceptance package should address the original failure path:

- structural interfaces seated within the applicable limits;
- left/right components confirmed in their intended positions;
- landing gear and crossbar free of unacceptable cracks, distortion, and looseness;
- buckle hook, cover, mating feature, and secondary engagement functioning under the approved check;
- tank mount and payload lock seating normally without forced alignment;
- cables correctly connected, retained, and clear of chafing or pinching;
- pads and plugs installed in the correct locations with underlying surfaces inspected;
- all required fastener checks completed using the specified data;
- app status and aircraft warnings reviewed;
- a complete DJI pre-flight inspection performed before operational release.

DJI's pre-flight checklist requires genuine components, secure mounting, firm cable connections, correctly secured spray tank and arm locks, and a damage-free, functioning payload system. The manual also instructs operators to examine app warnings before each flight. These aircraft-level checks are necessary, but they do not replace the repair-specific inspection and approval.

The manual states that general product maintenance should be performed every 100 flights or after more than 20 flight hours, while its recommended-maintenance table gives component-specific intervals such as monthly checks for aircraft-arm connectors and landing-gear fixing screws, routine buckle inspection, and a 12-month landing-gear crack inspection. Use the latest applicable schedule and event-driven inspections together. A recent scheduled check does not clear damage from a later impact.

## Practical answers to recurring ordering questions

**Can the numbered product image be used as the assembly sequence?** No. It identifies sales options but does not provide removal order, support points, torque, locking method, alignment, or inspection limits.

**Are the left and right frame connectors interchangeable?** The listing identifies separate right and left material numbers: `YC.JG.ZS005949` and `YC.JG.ZS005948`. No interchangeability should be assumed.

**Do callouts 3 and 14 represent different screws?** The public listing gives both the same material number, `YC.ST.LL000730`, and the same screw description, but different store SKU suffixes. Preserve the positional references and obtain seller confirmation rather than inventing a difference.

**Is a broken buckle-hook cover enough reason to order only callout 13?** Not by itself. Inspect the buckle hook, mating engagement, connector geometry, and evidence of the event that damaged the cover. The hook is a separate callout 15.

**Can a loose fastener simply be tightened?** The catalog cannot answer that. The cause, mating-thread condition, contact surfaces, fastener identity, reuse rule, torque, and locking process require approved maintenance data.

**Does the 2 mm limit apply to every frame gap?** No. The user manual states it for the gap between the aircraft arm and aircraft-arm connector in its scheduled maintenance table. Apply limits only to the component and measurement defined by the source.

**Can a visually straight landing-gear crossbar be released after a hard landing?** Visual appearance alone is insufficient. Inspect the complete load path and use the applicable dimensional and damage limits from approved service information.
