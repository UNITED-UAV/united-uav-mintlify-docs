An electronic speed controller should be selected as part of a propulsion system, not by matching the ampere value in its product name to a motor's maximum-current line. The battery, connector, power-distribution path, ESC, motor, propeller, airframe cooling, command signal, mission profile, and failure response all interact. A controller can remain below a headline current rating and still overheat because of poor airflow, excessive switching loss, long high-current leads, repeated acceleration, or an unsuitable motor-propeller combination.

This guide applies that system approach to the [UNITED UAV T-MOTOR FLAME 70A 6S listing](https://store.uniteduav.com/products/flame-70a-6s?utm_source=mintlify&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=mintlify_flame_70a_6s_guide_20260927). It is written for UAV builders, repair teams, propulsion integrators, and technical buyers who need a traceable selection and commissioning record. It does not replace the current T-MOTOR manual, aircraft electrical design, test plan, or airworthiness decision.

## Resolve the published current-rating conflict first

The first engineering issue is source control. The current UNITED UAV product description states a 35 A continuous-current reference and a 70 A peak reference. The current [T-MOTOR manufacturer product page](https://store.tmotor.com/product/flame-70a-6s-esc.html) states 70 A continuous and 80 A peak for 10 seconds. These values are not equivalent, and an integrator should not silently choose the more convenient set.

Treat the discrepancy as a configuration gate:

1. Record the exact model name, physical label, connector and lead configuration, packaging label, purchase date, store SKU, and any batch or revision marking.
2. Ask the supplier or manufacturer to identify the specification that applies to the shipped hardware.
3. Retain the response, current datasheet, and photographs in the aircraft build record.
4. Base the electrical and thermal qualification on the confirmed limit and its stated test conditions.
5. If the revision cannot be resolved, use the lower defensible limit for preliminary risk assessment and do not approve the aircraft for flight on an assumption.

The product name “70A” is not a complete rating definition. A valid rating needs duration, input voltage, cooling condition, ambient temperature, conductor configuration, and allowable temperature rise. The manufacturer's 80 A figure is explicitly time-limited to 10 seconds on its current page. It must not be treated as a continuous operating point, and a flight profile that repeatedly reaches the peak can produce cumulative heating even when every individual event is shorter than 10 seconds.

This source conflict also means that procurement, engineering, and maintenance must reference the same controlled specification. Otherwise, purchasing may order against the current website, design may use an older table, and technicians may accept replacement hardware based only on the enclosure label.

## Treat the CW and CCW options as unresolved configuration data

The UNITED UAV listing exposes CW and CCW variants with different SKUs. Three-phase brushless motor direction is often changed through phase connection or controller configuration, but that general fact does not prove what these two commercial options represent. They may refer to prepared phase order, labeling, a motor pairing convention, or inventory organization. The current T-MOTOR manufacturer page presents one FLAME 70A 6S product and does not document separate CW and CCW hardware on the public page.

Before ordering, ask for a written answer to four questions:

- Is the hardware or firmware different between the CW and CCW variants?
- Are phase wires terminated or ordered differently?
- Does either option impose a motor-direction or propeller-direction requirement?
- Can motor direction be changed during integration without affecting warranty or protection behavior?

Record the selected SKU against the aircraft motor position. Do not use a CW/CCW label as a substitute for a motor-direction test with propellers removed. Conversely, do not assume two differently labeled units are interchangeable merely because they look identical.

## Build the propulsion load case before selecting the ESC

The correct input is measured or manufacturer-supported current for the exact motor, propeller, battery voltage, and operating environment. Motor KV alone is insufficient. Propeller diameter, pitch, blade count, air density, motor timing, supply voltage, mechanical condition, and command transients can change current substantially.

Create a load-case table for each intended configuration:

- motor model, KV, winding or revision;
- propeller model, diameter, pitch, blade count, and direction;
- battery chemistry, cell count, capacity, discharge capability, and connector;
- minimum and maximum operating voltage;
- expected takeoff mass and mission phase;
- steady hover current per motor;
- maximum sustained current and duration;
- transient current, duration, and repetition rate;
- ambient temperature, altitude, and expected cooling airflow;
- measured ESC case temperature and location of the sensor;
- source and date for every value.

For example, a propulsion test showing 45 A at one motor under a particular 6S propeller combination does not simply create “25 A of margin” against a 70 A number. The integrator must still evaluate sensor accuracy, unit-to-unit variation, voltage at the ESC, hot-day performance, propeller manufacturing tolerance, maneuver transients, blocked airflow, contamination, and how long the 45 A condition persists.

The battery and distribution system must be sized for the aircraft total, not one ESC. A four-motor aircraft drawing 45 A per motor represents 180 A before avionics and margin. An eight-motor aircraft at the same per-motor point represents 360 A. These arithmetic examples do not predict actual flight current; they show why a correctly sized individual ESC can still be connected to an undersized battery, connector, fuse, bus, or distribution board.

Voltage must be treated just as carefully. A conventional 6S LiPo reaches 25.2 V when each cell is charged to 4.2 V. That calculated maximum belongs in insulation, capacitor, connector, and test-equipment review. Nominal voltage alone is not the worst-case input. Confirm the battery's actual chemistry and charge limit rather than applying 25.2 V blindly to every product described as 6S.

## Design the high-current path as one controlled assembly

The electrical path includes the battery cell tabs, pack leads, main connector, anti-spark device if fitted, fuse or protection device, power-distribution conductors, branch connector, ESC input leads, ESC switching stage, phase leads, motor windings, and all joints between them. Heat or voltage drop at any one point can disturb the ESC even if the controller itself is healthy.

For every conductor and connector, document:

- continuous and transient current requirement;
- voltage rating and insulation system;
- wire material, cross-section, strand construction, and temperature rating;
- connector type, contact rating, mating-cycle condition, and polarity control;
- crimp or solder process and inspection criteria;
- mechanical support and strain relief;
- routing relative to sharp edges, moving parts, heat, chemicals, and high-vibration zones;
- maximum permitted length under the manufacturer's integration guidance.

Long battery leads add inductance. Rapid ESC switching can create voltage overshoot at the controller input. Do not solve this by adding arbitrary capacitors or extending motor leads without an approved design. Obtain the manufacturer's cable-length and capacitor guidance for the exact revision. Any added capacitor needs the correct voltage rating, ripple-current capability, equivalent series resistance, mounting, insulation, polarity control, and mechanical support.

Minimize unsupported mass at solder joints. A cable should not use an ESC pad or connector pin as its strain relief. Protect conductors from carbon-fiber edges and conductive structure. Where a high-current cable passes near the command lead, preserve separation and avoid large loops. If the design requires crossing, a controlled crossing geometry is preferable to long parallel routing.

## Control soldering and termination quality

A joint can look shiny and still be electrically or mechanically unacceptable. Define the solder alloy, flux, tip size, temperature window, dwell time, wetting requirement, insulation setback, heat-shrink coverage, and pull-inspection method appropriate to the supplied leads and connectors. Prevent solder from wicking far into flexible cable, which can create a rigid transition that concentrates vibration stress.

Inspect for:

- incomplete wetting, voids, spikes, bridges, or exposed strands;
- heat-damaged insulation or softened connector housings;
- reversed polarity or inconsistent phase labeling;
- conductive debris trapped against the aluminum enclosure or PCB entry;
- insufficient creepage between exposed conductors;
- heat shrink that conceals an uninspected joint;
- cable tension transferred to the ESC body.

Use independent polarity verification before first power. A color convention is useful, but it is not proof. Verify from the battery interface to the ESC input using the aircraft wiring record and suitable instruments. Keep the propulsion battery disconnected during continuity checks and mechanical work.

## Verify the command interface instead of assuming “PWM compatible”

The manufacturer's current product page states a 500 Hz refresh rate. That does not fully specify the interface. The flight controller must provide the correct electrical level, ground reference, pulse range, update rate, startup state, and failsafe behavior for the exact ESC revision.

Record these items before energizing the motor:

- signal type and electrical level;
- required signal ground connection;
- minimum, maximum, and neutral or disarmed command values;
- whether throttle calibration is required, permitted, or fixed;
- arming tones or status indications and their meanings;
- behavior after signal loss, invalid input, or controller reboot;
- startup timing relative to the flight controller;
- whether the ESC provides telemetry and, if so, its protocol and wiring;
- firmware version and any programmable settings.

Do not import pulse widths, CAN settings, or programming procedures from another T-MOTOR family. A V-series document, an ALPHA-series controller, and a FLAME-series product may use different interfaces or protection behavior. A connector that fits does not establish the pinout.

Signal-loss testing is an aircraft safety test, not merely an electronics check. Determine what the propulsion channel does when the command wire opens, the flight controller reboots, the reference ground is lost, or the update stream freezes. Perform the test in a controlled configuration with propellers removed or with an engineered restraint and exclusion zone.

## Engineer cooling around the actual mission

The CNC aluminum enclosure and layered construction can move heat away from internal components, but the enclosure still needs a path to the environment. Cooling performance changes with mounting orientation, airflow, neighboring heat sources, protective covers, dust screens, chemical residue, solar loading, and whether the aircraft is hovering or stationary.

A ground test can be thermally conservative because the aircraft is not moving, but it can also be misleading if a bench fan produces airflow that the installed ESC never receives. Propeller wash may improve cooling in flight, while an airframe fairing, payload, folded cable bundle, or contamination layer may block it. Qualify the installed condition.

Use a test matrix that includes:

- cold start and hot restart;
- minimum and maximum battery voltage;
- representative continuous load;
- defined acceleration and deceleration cycles;
- maximum planned transient with its repetition rate;
- highest intended ambient temperature;
- reduced-density or altitude condition where relevant;
- clean installation and the allowable contaminated condition;
- one-motor and full-aircraft effects on airflow and bus voltage.

Record ambient temperature, ESC case temperature at a defined point, motor temperature, current, voltage at the ESC, command, RPM if available, test duration, and the time to thermal stabilization. Case temperature is not the same as semiconductor junction temperature. Use manufacturer limits and a justified correlation rather than inventing an internal temperature from an infrared reading.

## Environmental protection is an aircraft-level responsibility

T-MOTOR describes a waterproof design for this FLAME model on its series page, but the cited public pages do not provide a specific IP rating for the exact FLAME 70A 6S unit. Even when an enclosure resists water, connectors, cut wire ends, solder joints, programming leads, cable entries, and mounting interfaces can remain vulnerable.

For agricultural, maritime, inspection, or all-weather work, define exposure to:

- liquid water, condensation, and washdown;
- fertilizer, pesticide, salt, dust, and conductive residue;
- ultraviolet radiation and temperature cycling;
- vibration, impact, and cable flexing;
- galvanic interaction between the aluminum enclosure and mounting hardware;
- drainage paths and trapped moisture.

Do not bury the ESC in sealant or a closed box without thermal analysis and manufacturer approval. Added protection can trap heat, stress cable entries, prevent inspection, and interfere with pressure equalization. After exposure, inspect connectors and joints for residue, corrosion, tracking, softened insulation, and loss of strain relief.

## Use a staged commissioning plan

Commissioning should progress from identity control to low-energy verification and then to representative load. Each stage has a stop condition.

### Stage 1: receiving and identity inspection

Verify the label, SKU, CW/CCW option, package contents, dimensions, mass where controlled, lead configuration, enclosure condition, and revision evidence. Compare the unit with the order and the specification response that resolved the current-rating conflict. Quarantine unexplained differences.

### Stage 2: unpowered aircraft inspection

Complete the wiring record. Verify polarity independently, phase labeling, connector engagement, insulation, mounting, clearance, strain relief, and separation from moving or sharp components. Measure for unintended shorts using a method appropriate to the aircraft electronics. Do not rely on a continuity beep alone when capacitors and parallel electronics affect the reading.

### Stage 3: controlled first energization

Use an approved current-limited or protected power-up method if compatible with the propulsion system. Keep propellers removed. Confirm normal startup indications, absence of smoke or odor, stable input current, and correct flight-controller communication. Stop on unexpected current, repeated tones, heating, or intermittent startup.

### Stage 4: command and direction verification

With propellers removed, test arming, low-command response, smooth speed increase, motor direction, stop command, signal loss, and controller reboot behavior. Confirm every motor position against the aircraft direction diagram. Do not treat the purchased CW/CCW option as proof of rotation.

### Stage 5: restrained propulsion test

Install the specified propeller only in a test system designed for the expected thrust, torque, fragment energy, and electrical power. Establish an exclusion zone and emergency shutdown. Instrument input voltage, current, command, RPM, and temperatures. Reproduce the intended sustained and transient conditions without exceeding the confirmed limits.

### Stage 6: full-system validation

Test all propulsion channels together to expose battery sag, connector heating, distribution imbalance, electromagnetic interference, and cooling interactions. Repeat critical points after thermal soak. Review data for unit-to-unit current difference, unstable RPM, reset events, and temperature divergence.

### Stage 7: post-test inspection and release

Power down and make the system safe. Inspect connectors, solder joints, insulation, cable support, enclosure mounting, odor, discoloration, residue, and looseness. Save the raw data, test configuration, photographs, instruments, calibration status, results, anomalies, and technical approval. A successful spin test without records is not a qualification baseline.

## Diagnose symptoms at system level

| Symptom | Evidence to capture | Competing causes to separate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ESC will not arm | input voltage at ESC, startup indication, command waveform, ground reference, firmware/settings | invalid endpoints, missing ground, startup order, signal type, protection state, wiring error |
| Motor stutters at startup | phase continuity, connector/joint condition, motor resistance comparison, command ramp, load | poor phase joint, damaged motor, unsuitable timing, low bus voltage, mechanical drag |
| Motor stops or loses synchronization at high load | current, bus voltage at ESC, RPM, command, temperature, propeller, event timing | voltage sag, current limit, motor-prop mismatch, phase fault, overheating, control setting |
| ESC becomes hotter than peer units | per-channel current, airflow, mounting, joint resistance, motor/prop identity | load imbalance, blocked cooling, resistive connector, damaged motor, different hardware revision |
| Flight controller resets or sensors become noisy | bus transients, grounds, cable routing, power-rail data, event correlation | conducted or radiated interference, distribution impedance, ground shift, inadequate avionics supply |
| Intermittent operation after vibration | connector retention, strain relief, cable movement, solder joints, logs | fractured conductor, fretting contact, cold joint, pin damage, enclosure or PCB damage |
| One motor draws more current at equal command | thrust/RPM, propeller identity, motor condition, phase joints, mechanical alignment | propeller variance, motor damage, bearing drag, ESC variance, airflow interaction |

Do not replace the ESC solely because it is the component reporting or experiencing the symptom. A controller can be the failed item, but it can also be reacting correctly to a battery, motor, phase, signal, cooling, or mechanical problem. Preserve logs and reproduce only under a controlled test plan.

## Create a reusable acceptance baseline

For each propulsion position, keep a commissioning record containing:

- aircraft and motor-position identifier;
- ESC model, label, revision, SKU, and CW/CCW option;
- confirmed continuous and peak current specification with source and date;
- motor and propeller identity;
- battery and distribution configuration;
- connector and wire specification;
- firmware and command settings;
- ambient conditions and test setup;
- current, voltage, RPM, command, and temperature traces;
- signal-loss and restart results;
- photographs before and after the load test;
- final inspection and release approval.

This baseline makes later troubleshooting objective. A technician can compare current or temperature against the aircraft's own qualified state rather than against a generic internet value. Trend changes matter: increasing connector temperature, widening current imbalance, delayed startup, new desynchronization, or insulation discoloration can justify removal before a complete failure.

Define maintenance checks for secure mounting, enclosure damage, lead abrasion, connector retention, joint discoloration, chemical residue, corrosion, and thermal evidence. Repeat the relevant commissioning tests after changing the motor, propeller, battery voltage, ESC firmware, flight-controller output, cable length, connector, cooling arrangement, payload, or mission duty cycle.
