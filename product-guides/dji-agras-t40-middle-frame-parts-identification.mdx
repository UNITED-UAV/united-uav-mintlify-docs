The DJI Agras T40 middle-frame parts listing is not a single replacement assembly. It is a collection of separately selectable components shown against one numbered, laid-out parts image. That distinction matters when an aircraft has been damaged: a visible bend, loose arm connection, abraded cable, or damaged sensor mount can involve several adjacent parts, but the photograph alone does not establish which components are reusable. The aim of this guide is to turn an uncertain repair request into an unambiguous identification and purchasing record. It is **not** a structural repair procedure, a torque specification, or an authorization to return an aircraft to flight.

## Read the three identifiers in the right order

A robust request identifies **the aircraft and its configuration**, **the diagram callout**, and **the complete material number**. Add the part name as a fourth cross-check. For example, “T40, callout 2, `YC.JG.QX002193`, M4 Aircraft Arm Connector” is substantially clearer than “the black arm fitting.” The latter description is ambiguous because the listing separately names M1, M2, M3, and M4 connectors. Even if two pieces appear similar in a small photograph, their different material numbers are a reason to verify rather than assume interchangeability.

The diagram number answers “which object in this image?” The material number answers “which cataloged component?” The store's selectable variant answers “which option will be placed in the cart?” These answers should agree before checkout. A diagram number alone can be reused in another T40 parts image, and a generic English name may cover several positions. Conversely, a material number can appear at more than one position in the same image. Never use any one field as a substitute for the other two.

Record the product-page URL and the date on which the option was checked. Catalog variants, availability, packaging, and descriptions can change. A screenshot showing the selected option is useful during a quotation, but the live selected variant remains the final ordering reference. If a physical part bears a legible marking, photograph it and compare every character; do not match only a numeric suffix. If there is no marking, identify the part through its installed location, nearby components, connector geometry, and the numbered image, then ask for confirmation before paying.

## Arm connectors: identify a position, not a silhouette

The four arm-connector entries are labeled M1 through M4, each with its own material number. Their appearance in the laid-out image is helpful for recognizing a family of parts, but the picture does not tell a technician how to orient an assembled aircraft. Before calling a piece “left,” “front,” or “M3,” establish the reference orientation from the aircraft's approved documentation and record the actual arm marking or installed position. A photograph taken from behind or beneath the airframe can reverse the apparent left/right relationship; a parts order based on that photographic viewpoint is vulnerable to error.

For a suspected connector problem, take one overview image showing the complete aircraft and marked arm, a second image showing the connector in its installed position, and close-ups of both mating faces and fastener holes after an authorized inspection. Look for visible cracking, deformation, missing material, irregular gaps, fretting, elongated holes, and signs that adjacent wiring has been pinched. These observations are **triage evidence**, not a declaration that a connector is structurally sound. The absence of a visible crack in a photograph cannot rule out hidden damage or justify reuse after an impact.

Do not collapse the arm connector and the arm fixing piece into one purchase request. The listing separately names M1/M2/M3/M4 connectors and left/right fixing pieces. If an impact affected both, a technician should identify each separately against the physical aircraft and the numbered reference. The same caution applies to the neighboring aluminum tube and insert: damage can propagate through an interface, so ordering only the most visibly broken part may leave the actual repair scope unresolved.

## Side-specific tubes and the limits of a lay-flat image

Callout 5 is the **Middle Frame Aluminum Tube (Right)**, material `BC.AG.SS000579`; callout 6 is the **Middle Frame Aluminum Tube (Left)**, material `BC.AG.SS000578`. The two material numbers differ by one digit. That makes a complete-code comparison essential, especially when an order is dictated over the phone or copied from a cropped screenshot. The word “right” in the catalog name should be reconciled with the aircraft's own orientation convention, not the viewer's right side of a product photograph.

The product image shows loose parts arranged for identification. It does not show the installed load path, joint preparation, mating surfaces, or acceptable dimensional tolerances. Therefore, a field note such as “tube seems straight” is not a sufficient structural acceptance criterion. Record where a bend, dent, gouge, or connection displacement is seen; compare the part with its matching catalog entry; and route the airframe to a qualified repair process. Do not straighten, drill, shim, bond, or substitute a frame member on the basis of this guide. The listing provides no approved dimensions, repair limits, or torque sequence for doing so.

Callout 10, `YC.JG.QX002110`, is labeled **Middle Frame Insert Part**. Its name indicates a different catalog item from either tube. If an insert is loose, absent, or visibly distorted, include it as a separate line in the inspection record instead of assuming it is supplied with a tube. Likewise, if a tube is being replaced, ask whether associated inserts, connectors, fasteners, covers, or clips are included in the sales unit. The listing does not establish a bundled kit for these positions.

## Separate the load-sensor module from its supporting hardware

Four entries in the image's load-sensor area have materially different functions in an order: the **Load Sensor Module** at callout 9 (`BC.AG.SS000550`), the **Weight Sensor Module Fixing Piece** at 13 (`YC.JG.BJ000942`), the **Weighing Sensor Rubber Cover** at 14 (`YC.JG.MY001011.05`), and the **Load Sensor Cable Clip** at 18 (`YC.JG.ZS002229`). A buyer who requests “the sensor part” has not specified which of these is damaged. A module may be physically intact while its fixing piece or cable retention is damaged; the reverse is also possible. The catalog names alone do not diagnose either condition.

Capture the mounted sensor area before disturbing it. Include the module body and label, the bracket or fixing piece, the rubber cover, the cable path, and both sides of every accessible connector in separate photographs. Note whether a fault appeared after impact, after washing, after an earlier repair, or without an obvious external event. Such history is useful to a qualified technician, but it should not be converted into an unsupported conclusion about electrical calibration or sensor accuracy.

The protective and retention parts are not cosmetic in an identification exercise. A damaged rubber cover can change what is exposed to dirt and moisture; a displaced cable clip can allow movement or abrasion. That is a reason to inspect their condition and routing, not a claim that replacing either will clear a fault. The T40 flight manual's pre-flight checklist asks operators to ensure parts are secure and cables are connected properly. It does not provide, in the cited section, a middle-frame load-sensor calibration procedure. Any post-repair sensor test or calibration should therefore follow the relevant approved service information, not an invented sequence in a purchasing article.

## Fasteners: one base material number, several callouts

Fastener ordering is the most error-prone part of this listing. `YC.ST.LL000013` is named **Screw M40-HC01400140-070039-0003-Y** and appears at callouts 3, 12, 21, and 25. The image shows visually separate screw groups, while the product variants attach different store SKUs to the repeated callouts. A second screw, **M30-HC100100-55-85**, has material number `YC.WJ.L00871` at callout 16; its store SKU also carries a suffix. These identifiers should be copied exactly rather than shortened to “M4 screw” or “M3 screw.” A nominal thread-size guess is not an order specification.

Before purchasing, ask three separate questions: What is the **material number** printed in the catalog? Which **numbered variant** is being selected? What **unit and quantity** will actually ship for that selection? Then ask the servicing technician for the number of new fasteners required by the approved repair. Neither the product photo nor the quantity field supplies a torque value, tightening pattern, re-use rule, thread-locking requirement, or substitute specification. Applying an assumed torque or using an apparently similar shop fastener would go beyond the information available here.

This matters even when a screw looks undamaged. A service instruction may require replacement after removal, or may specify a particular fastening compound or sequence; this public catalog page does not answer those questions. Keep the removed hardware associated with its original location for identification, but do not re-use it merely because it fits. If the approved service documentation cannot be obtained, escalate the work rather than reverse-engineering an airworthy fastening decision from a sales image.

## Routing parts: small pieces with distinct identification roles

The **Cable Tube** at callout 1 (`YC.JG.QX002085`), **Hose Clip** at 15 (`YC.JG.ZS002259`), and **Cable Tube Rubber Stopper** at 20 (`YC.JG.MY000686`) are separate selectable items. Their presence in the same middle-frame image does not make them interchangeable with one another or with the load-sensor cable clip at 18. When an aircraft has cable or hose abrasion near the frame, document the exact retention point and the material passing through it. A displaced clip can be a symptom of another problem, such as a deformed support or incorrect prior routing, rather than the whole repair.

Photograph the route before moving anything. Include an overview showing the path across the frame and detail shots of each transition, support, stopper, and nearby moving or folding interface. Note evidence of rubbing, compression, sharp bends, stretched cable, damaged insulation, hose wear, or missing retention. Do not pull a cable tighter to make it reach a clip or infer a hose path from the lay-flat product image. The final route and clearances must come from the correct aircraft configuration and approved service information.

## A seven-step identification workflow for a real repair request

1. **Establish the machine record.** Record “DJI Agras T40,” the aircraft identifier used by the service organization, its relevant configuration, the incident or symptom, and the person performing the inspection. Keep T40 and T20P material searches separate even though DJI publishes a combined flight manual; a combined manual is not evidence that their frame parts interchange.
2. **Make the aircraft safe for inspection.** Follow the applicable DJI handling guidance. The T40/T20P manual instructs that the aircraft be powered off and the flight battery removed for handling and disassembly. A technician should also control the surrounding work area and any spray-system residue under the site procedure. Do not inspect a suspected structural fault with the aircraft ready to start.
3. **Capture context before detail.** Take an all-aircraft view, then a frame-area view, then close-ups. Include a scale reference only if it does not obscure the part. Preserve original image orientation and label the viewpoint; otherwise a later reviewer may swap left and right.
4. **Match the numbered image.** Find the closest callout on the UNITED UAV page, but do not stop there. If the image label has no selectable variant, record that as an unresolved catalog gap. If the suspected part is a screw, note every related callout instead of treating repeated material numbers as independent designs.
5. **Match the exact option.** Confirm the full material number, the English name, and the selected variant title. For repeated material numbers, capture the store SKU and ask what differentiates the sales options. Confirm what is included, the quantity per option, and whether an adjacent part is sold separately.
6. **Have a qualified technician define repair scope.** The technician, not the catalog page, determines which parts require replacement and which checks follow installation. If there is structural deformation, sensor fault, damaged wiring, or uncertainty about a fastening requirement, keep the aircraft out of service until an approved repair decision exists.
7. **Close with a verifiable record.** Keep the approved parts list, supplier confirmation, received-part labels, installation record, tests, and release decision together. A purchase receipt proves acquisition; it does not prove compatibility, correct installation, or airworthiness.

## Example: preparing a quotation without inventing a repair

Suppose an operator reports a visible gap at one arm interface and a cable rubbing near the middle frame. An inadequate request would be “send one T40 arm connector and some clips.” A useful request would identify the arm position using the aircraft's orientation reference, attach an overview and close-ups, identify the probable connector callout and full material number, identify the exact retention part if its callout can be confirmed, and state that the repair scope remains subject to technician inspection. If the arm position is not yet established, the correct line item is **“connector identification pending,”** not a guessed M1/M2/M3/M4 variant.

A quotation worksheet can have columns for observed condition, diagram number, material number, selected store option, requested sales quantity, package-unit confirmation, and reviewer approval. For a sensor-area issue, add separate rows for the module, fixing piece, rubber cover, and cable clip only when each is actually implicated. Blank fields are preferable to confident-looking guesses. This is especially true for quantities: the diagram's counts and the number of parts needed to repair one aircraft are different concepts. If the seller quotes an assembly instead of a loose component, record that substitution explicitly and have the technician approve it before purchase.

## Receiving and return-to-service: two different gates

On receipt, check the supplier's label against the approved order line: full material number, selected variant, name, side or arm position, quantity, and any revision or kit contents shown on the shipment documents. Photograph the item and packaging before installation if the part is hard to distinguish later. Inspect for obvious transit damage and packaging discrepancies. A part that looks similar but has a different code should be quarantined for clarification; do not “test fit” it as a substitute without approval.

After a qualified repair, use the relevant current DJI documentation and the organization's maintenance procedure for installation checks. As an aircraft-level final gate, DJI's v1.4 manual calls for secure parts and cables, secure propellers, unfolded arms, and firmly tightened arm locks before flight. Its post-flight inspection addresses structure, dirt, loose or damaged parts, folding for transport, and battery-connector condition. Those are useful categories for a release checklist, but they are **not** a substitute for component-specific measurements, torque requirements, sensor verification, or a documented maintenance sign-off. A parts article cannot establish that a repaired aircraft is safe to fly.

The separation between these gates is deliberate. A correct product variant can still be installed incorrectly; a neat-looking assembly can still have an unresolved sensor or structural issue; and a successful power-on is not a structural acceptance test. Conversely, a rejected part need not mean the entire aircraft requires a new frame. Record the reason for acceptance or rejection at each stage so that purchasing, service, and operations can trace the decision without reconstructing it from chat messages.

## Practical answers to common selection questions

**Are the left and right aluminum tubes the same part?** No such interchangeability is established by the listing. It provides distinct left and right names and distinct material numbers, `BC.AG.SS000578` and `BC.AG.SS000579`. Use the side-specific option confirmed for the actual aircraft.

**Can I order by an M1–M4 arm name alone?** The names help narrow the search, but a responsible order also includes the full material number, diagram callout, aircraft position, and selected variant. A photograph of a loose connector without location context may not be enough.

**Does callout 3 mean I should order 73 screws?** No. The page says 73 is the quantity shown in its diagram, while the same base material number appears under other callouts with zero shown. Confirm the repair quantity and the supplier's sales unit rather than converting a diagram count into a purchase order.

**What if the part I need is numbered in the picture but absent from the menu?** Do not choose a neighboring variant. Provide the marked picture, aircraft context, and the missing callout to UNITED UAV and request an exact material-number or assembly-level answer.

**Will replacing the load-sensor rubber cover solve a sensor error?** The catalog does not support that diagnosis. The cover, fixing piece, cable clip, and module are distinct items. A qualified technician must identify the fault and the correct post-repair test.
